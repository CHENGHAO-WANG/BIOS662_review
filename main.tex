\section{Study designs}

\begin{frame}{Parallel group design}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}

      Each participant is randomized to one treatment and remains on it.

      \[
        \text{Group 1: } A \qquad \text{Group 2: } B
      \]
      A common estimand is the between-group mean difference
      \[
        \Delta = \E(Y\mid A)-\E(Y\mid B).
      \]
    \column{0.42\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Pros}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Simple analysis and interpretation
          \item No carryover between treatments
          \item Takes shorter time than crossover design
          \item Suitable for permanent or lasting effects
        \end{itemize}
      \end{exampleblock}
      \begin{alertblock}{Cons}
        Between-person variability contributes to the variance.
      \end{alertblock}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Crossover design}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.54\textwidth}
      Participants receive multiple treatments in randomized sequences:
      \[
        \text{Sequence 1: } A \longrightarrow B,
        \qquad
        \text{Sequence 2: } B \longrightarrow A.
      \]
      With two periods, a natural contrast is within participant:
      \[
        \Delta = \E(Y_A-Y_B).
      \]
      Each participant serves as their own control, eliminating
      subject-to-subject variability.

      Why randomizing the sequence? To adjust for period effects.
    \column{0.43\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{exampleblock}{Pros}
        Removes much between-subject variation, often increasing efficiency.
      \end{exampleblock}
      \vspace{-0.4em}
      \begin{alertblock}{Cons}
        \begin{itemize}
          \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
          \item Carryover effects: the effect of an earlier treatment persists
                into a later period.
          \item Calendar-time effects: outcome changes due to real-world time rather than
                the treatment.
          \item The treatment introduces permanent changes to the experimental units.
          \item Takes longer time than parallel group design:
                the chance of dropout increases; the investigator and subject
                enthusiasm wanes.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{alertblock}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel or crossover?}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.33\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.33\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Parallel} & \textbf{Crossover} \\
    \midrule
    Best fit & Lasting treatment effects; unstable disease & Reversible effects; stable condition \\
    Comparison & Between participants & Within participants \\
    Main concern & Baseline heterogeneity & Carryover and period effects \\
    Washout & Not needed & Needed when residual effects are plausible \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{exampleblock}{Example}
    For something like blood-pressure drugs with short-lived effects, crossover can be useful.
    For surgery, vaccines, cancer treatment, or therapies that permanently change disease course,
    parallel design is usually a better choice.
  \end{exampleblock}

  \textbf{Check:} Why can increasing the washout period reduce carryover but increase dropout?
\end{frame}

\section{Clinical-trial phases}

\begin{frame}{Clinical-trial phases I and II}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Phase I: safety and dose}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Primary focus: safe dosage range, side effects, and pharmacokinetics
          \item If possible, gain early evidence of effectiveness
          \item Usually a small group of people (20-80)
          \item Often healthy volunteers; sometimes patients
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Phase II: preliminary efficacy}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Effectiveness plus continued safety assessment
          \item A larger group of people (100-300)
          \item Patients with the condition of interest
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  \alert{The phase describes the development objective, not merely the sample size.}

  Cell/lab studies and animal studies are performed before the clinical trial (pre-clinical research).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Clinical-trial phases III and IV}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Phase III: confirmatory evaluation}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Further evaluation of effectiveness and safety
          \item Larger populations (1000-3000) and in different regions and countries
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Phase IV: after approval}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item After approval
          \item Long-term outcomes and uncommon adverse events
          \item In the general population over a longer timeframe
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{alertblock}{Classification is not always clean}
    The phase framework is most natural for drug development. Trials of behavioral,
    surgical, or already-approved interventions may not fit one phase exactly.
  \end{alertblock}
  \textbf{Check:} A large definitive trial of an already-approved device resembles
  Phase III in purpose, but why might the label be debatable?
\end{frame}

\section{Bootstrap sample size}

\begin{frame}{Three quantities that may be easy to confuse}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.19\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.36\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{What it controls} \\
    \midrule
    Original \(n\) & Number of independent observations collected & Statistical information about the population \\
    Resample size \(n^*\) & Observations drawn with replacement in one bootstrap sample & Usually set to \(n\) to mimic sampling from the empirical distribution \\
    Replicates \(B\) & Number of bootstrap samples generated & Monte Carlo stability of the bootstrap calculation \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  Increasing \(B\) reduces simulation noise at approximately the rate \(B^{-1/2}\),
  but it does \alert{not} add information to the original study.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How large should \(B\) be?}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The course examples use \(B=500\), which is enough to demonstrate the method.
    \item Other common choices of \(B\): 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000.
    \item Compute \(B\) using the error rate \(B^{-1/2}\).
  \end{itemize}
  \begin{alertblock}{What increasing \(B\) cannot fix}
    Bias, small original sample sizen $n$, a poor bootstrap approximation
    (for statistics such as extreme quantiles).
  \end{alertblock}
  \textbf{Check:} If \(n=21\) and \(B=2000\), how many observations are in each
  ordinary nonparametric bootstrap sample?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A bootstrap algorithm}
  Given observations \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) and statistic
  \(\widehat\theta=t(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\):
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item For \(b=1,\ldots,B\), sample \(n\) observations with replacement from
          \(\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}\).
    \item Compute the statistic \(\widehat\theta^{*(b)}\) from bootstrap sample \(b\).
    \item Use the empirical distribution
          \(\widehat\theta^{*(1)},\ldots,\widehat\theta^{*(B)}\)
          to estimate uncertainty.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
  Different confidence intervals use these replicates in different ways.
\end{frame}

\section{Bootstrap confidence intervals}

\begin{frame}{Percentile bootstrap interval}
  Let \(Q_p^*\) be the \(p\) quantile of the bootstrap estimates
  \(\widehat\theta^{*(1)},\ldots,\widehat\theta^{*(B)}\).
  \begin{block}{A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) interval}
    \[
      \left[Q_{\alpha/2}^*,\ Q_{1-\alpha/2}^*\right].
    \]
  \end{block}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Use the middle \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) of the bootstrap estimates directly.
    \item For 95\% confidence, take their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Basic (reverse percentile) bootstrap interval}
  Let \(q_p^*\) be the \(p\) quantile of the bootstrap differences
  \(\widehat\theta^{*(b)}-\widehat\theta\), for \(b=1,\ldots,B\).
  \begin{block}{A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) interval}
    \[
      \left[\widehat\theta-q_{1-\alpha/2}^*,\quad
            \widehat\theta-q_{\alpha/2}^*\right].
    \]
  \end{block}
  Since \(q_p^*=Q_p^*-\widehat\theta\), where \(Q_p^*\) is the \(p\)
  quantile of the bootstrap estimates from the previous slide,
  this is equivalently
  \[
    \left[2\widehat\theta-Q_{1-\alpha/2}^*,\quad
          2\widehat\theta-Q_{\alpha/2}^*\right].
  \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bootstrap SE with a normal approximation}
  Estimate the standard error by the standard deviation of the
  bootstrap estimates:
  \[
    \widehat{\operatorname{se}}_{\rm boot}
      =\operatorname{sd}\!\left(\widehat\theta^{*(1)},\ldots,
                                   \widehat\theta^{*(B)}\right).
  \]
  \begin{block}{A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) interval}
    \[
      \widehat\theta\ \pm\ z_{1-\alpha/2}
                            \widehat{\operatorname{se}}_{\rm boot}.
    \]
  \end{block}
  For 95\% confidence, \(z_{0.975}\approx 1.96\). This method works best
  when the sampling distribution is approximately normal and bias is small.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bootstrap-\(t\) interval}
  Standardize each bootstrap estimate by its \emph{own} estimated SE:
  \[
    Z^{*(b)}=\frac{\widehat\theta^{*(b)}-\widehat\theta}
                    {\widehat{\operatorname{se}}^{*(b)}}.
  \]
  Let \(r_p^*\) be the \(p\) quantile of the \(Z^{*(b)}\)'s.
  \begin{block}{A \(100(1-\alpha)\%\) interval}
    \[
      \left[\widehat\theta-r_{1-\alpha/2}^*\widehat{\operatorname{se}},\quad
            \widehat\theta-r_{\alpha/2}^*\widehat{\operatorname{se}}\right],
    \]
    where \(\widehat{\operatorname{se}}\) is the SE from the original sample.
  \end{block}
  Bootstrap quantiles replace the usual \(t\) critical values;
  estimating an SE within every resample adds computation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exact versus large-sample methods}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.21\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}
                  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{Exact method} & \textbf{Large-sample method} \\
    \midrule
    Reference distribution & Finite-sample distribution under stated assumptions & Limiting approximation, often from a CLT \\
    Trade-off & May be conservative; useful when sample size is small; computationally harder & Simpler, but can be inaccurate in small samples \\

  For example, $np(1-p) \geq 10$ for binomial)
\end{frame}

\section{Confidence interval for a median}

\begin{frame}{Exact confidence interval for a median}
  Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be iid from a distribution with population
  median \(\zeta_{0.5}\), and let
  \(X_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(n)}\) denote the order statistics.

  The number of observations below the median satisfies $Binomial(n, 0.5)$.
  Choose the largest integer \(r\) satisfying
  \[
    \Pr\{\operatorname{Binomial}(n,0.5)\leq r-1\}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}.
  \]
  Then a symmetric distribution-free interval is
  \[
    {\left(X_{(r)},\;X_{(n-r+1)}\right)}.
  \]
  Its actual coverage is
  \[
    \Pr\{X_{(r)}\leq\zeta_{0.5}\leq X_{(n-r+1)}\}\geq 1-\alpha.
  \]
  Discreteness is why the coverage can be greater than the nominal level.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example: \(n=23\)}
  For a 95\% interval, choose \(r=7\) because
  \[
    \Pr\{\operatorname{Binomial}(23,0.5)\leq 6\}=0.01734\leq0.025,
  \]
  \[
    \Pr\{\operatorname{Binomial}(23,0.5)\leq 7\}=0.04657>0.025,
  \]
  whereas increasing \(r\) to 8 would make the tail probability too large.
  Therefore
  \[
    {\left(X_{(7)},\;X_{(17)}\right)}
  \]
  is the exact interval.

  \begin{block}{Actual coverage}
    \[
      1-2(0.01734)=0.9653.
    \]
    The 96.53\% coverage exceeds 95\% because only discrete order-statistic choices
    are available.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parallel or crossover?} Decide whether within-person comparison is
          scientifically valid, not merely more efficient.
    \item \textbf{Trial phase?} Classify the development objective; recognize cases
          that do not map cleanly to the drug-development framework.
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap sample size?} Keep \(n\), \(n^*\), and \(B\) distinct. (Generally, \(n^*=n\))
    \item \textbf{Exact or large sample?} Identify the reference distribution and
          what is being approximated. Check the sample size.
    \item \textbf{Median CI?} Exact inference uses binomial tails and order
          statistics; large-sample inference approximates those tails.
  \end{enumerate}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boxplot recap: fence versus whisker}
  Let \(L\) and \(U\) denote the lower and upper edges of the box.
  \[
    \operatorname{IQR}=U-L,
    \qquad
    F_L=L-1.5\operatorname{IQR},
    \qquad
    F_U=U+1.5\operatorname{IQR}.
  \]
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{What is drawn?}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item Box: from \(L\) to \(U\)
          \item Line inside the box: median
          \item Points beyond the whiskers: potential outliers
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Whisker endpoints}
        \[
          W_L=\min\{x_i:x_i\geq F_L\},
        \]
        \[
          W_U=\max\{x_i:x_i\leq F_U\}.
        \]
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \begin{alertblock}{Key distinction}
    A fence is a calculated cutoff; a whisker ends at an observed value.
    They are equal only if an observation happens to lie exactly on the fence.
  \end{alertblock}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Adding one observation: sum of squares}
  \small
  For the original \(n\geq2\) observations, define
  \[
    \bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\qquad
    Q=\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2=(n-1)s^2.
  \]
  Add \(X^*\), and write \(d=X^*-\bar X\). The new mean is
  \[
    \bar X_+=\frac{n\bar X+X^*}{n+1}=\bar X+\frac{d}{n+1}.
  \]
  Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)=0\), the cross term vanishes:
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    Q_+&=\sum_{i=1}^n\left[(X_i-\bar X)-\frac{d}{n+1}\right]^2
         +\left(\frac{nd}{n+1}\right)^2\\
       &=Q+\frac{nd^2}{(n+1)^2}+\frac{n^2d^2}{(n+1)^2}
        ={Q+\frac{n}{n+1}d^2}.
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  \alert{The sum of squares cannot decrease, but its variance denominator increases.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Adding one observation: when does variance increase?}
  \small
  With \(n+1\) observations, the new sample-variance denominator is \(n\):
  \[
    s_+^2=\frac{Q_+}{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}s^2+\frac{d^2}{n+1},
    \qquad
    s_+^2-s^2=\frac{d^2}{n+1}-\frac{s^2}{n}.
  \]
  Hence
  \[
    {s_+^2>s^2\ \Longleftrightarrow\
    |X^*-\bar X|>\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{n}}\,s.}
  \]
  Equality gives unchanged variance; a smaller distance gives decreased variance.
  \begin{alertblock}{Outside the observed range is not enough}
    Adding 11 to \(\{0,10\}\) gives \(\{0,10,11\}\),
    but sample variance decreases from 50 to 37.
    The relevant distance is from the mean, relative to the existing SD.
  \end{alertblock}
  \textbf{Notation:} \(n,\bar X,s\) all refer to the sample \emph{before addition}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Removing one observation: sum of squares}
  \small
  Start with \(n\geq3\) observations; let \(Q=(n-1)s^2\) and
  \(d_j=X_j-\bar X\). After removing \(X_j\),
  \[
    \bar X_- =\frac{n\bar X-X_j}{n-1}=\bar X-\frac{d_j}{n-1},
    \qquad Q_-=\sum_{i\ne j}(X_i-\bar X_-)^2.
  \]
  For the remaining observations,
  \(X_i-\bar X=(X_i-\bar X_-)-d_j/(n-1)\).
  Using \(\sum_{i\ne j}(X_i-\bar X_-)=0\),
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
    Q&=d_j^2+\sum_{i\ne j}
            \left[(X_i-\bar X_-)-\frac{d_j}{n-1}\right]^2\\
     &=d_j^2+Q_-+\frac{d_j^2}{n-1}
      =Q_-+\frac{n}{n-1}d_j^2.
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Therefore \({Q_-=(n-1)s^2-\frac{n}{n-1}d_j^2}\).
  \quad The new variance denominator is \(n-2\).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Removing one observation: when does variance decrease?}
  \small
  Divide the preceding sum of squares by \(n-2\), then subtract \(s^2\):
  \[
    s_-^2-s^2
    =\frac{(n-1)s^2-\frac{n}{n-1}d_j^2}{n-2}-s^2
    =\frac{s^2-\frac{n}{n-1}d_j^2}{n-2}.
  \]
  Since \(n-2>0\),
  \[
    {s_-^2<s^2\ \Longleftrightarrow\
    |X_j-\bar X|>\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}\,s.}
  \]
  Equality gives unchanged variance; a smaller distance gives increased variance.
  \begin{block}{What ``sufficiently extreme'' means}
    Distance from the original mean must exceed the threshold above.
    Being the minimum or maximum alone does not guarantee a decrease.
    SD changes in the same direction as variance.
  \end{block}
  \textbf{Notation:} \(n,\bar X,s\) refer to the sample \emph{before removal},
  including \(X_j\).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Variance of the sample mean}
  \small
  Suppose \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent observations with
  \(\E(X_i)=\mu\) and \(\Var(X_i)=\sigma^2\). Their sample mean is
  \[
    \bar X=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\qquad \E(\bar X)=\mu.
  \]
  Independence makes the covariance terms zero, so
  \[
    \Var(\bar X)
      =\Var\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right)
      =\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\Var(X_i)
      ={\frac{\sigma^2}{n}}.
  \]
  \begin{block}{Interpretation}
    Averaging \(n\) independent observations reduces variance by a factor
    of \(n\); the standard deviation of \(\bar X\) is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\).
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sample variance and estimated variance of \(\bar X\)}
  \small
  When the population variance \(\sigma^2\) is unknown, estimate it from
  the observations (\(n\geq2\)):
  \[
    s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2.
  \]
  Under independent, identically distributed sampling,
  \(\E(s^2)=\sigma^2\). Substitute \(s^2\) into \(\Var(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n\):
  \[
    {\widehat{\Var}(\bar X)=\frac{s^2}{n}},
    \qquad
    {\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)
      =\frac{s}{\sqrt n}}.
  \]
  \begin{alertblock}{Keep the targets distinct}
    \(s^2\) estimates the variance of individual observations;
    \(s^2/n\) estimates the variance of their sample mean.
  \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear transformations of a normal variable}
  \small
  If \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) and \(Y=a+bX\), then
  \[
    \E(Y)=a+b\mu,\qquad \Var(Y)=b^2\sigma^2.
  \]
  For \(b\neq0\), the transformed variable is also normal:
  \[
    {Y\sim N(a+b\mu,\;b^2\sigma^2)}.
  \]
  A negative \(b\) reverses direction but still gives a positive variance.
  If \(b=0\), then \(Y=a\) is constant.
  \begin{block}{Standardization}
    For \(\sigma>0\), take \(a=-\mu/\sigma\) and \(b=1/\sigma\).
    Then \(Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\sim N(0,1)\).
  \end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear transformations of a multivariate normal vector}
  \small
  Let \(\mathbf X\sim N_p(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\).
  For a fixed \(m\times p\) matrix \(A\) and a fixed vector \(\mathbf b\) with \(m\) entries,
  \[
    \E(A\mathbf X+\mathbf b)=A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b,\qquad
    \operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf X+\mathbf b)=A\boldsymbol\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}.
  \]
  Therefore,
  \[
    {\mathbf Y=A\mathbf X+\mathbf b
      \sim N_m(A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b,\;A\boldsymbol\Sigma A^{\mathsf T})}.
  \]
  \begin{block}{Application: the sample mean}
    If \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) are independent \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\),
    then \(\mathbf X\sim N_n(\mu\mathbf 1,\sigma^2 I_n)\).
    Taking \(A=\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}/n\) and \(\mathbf b=0\) gives
    \(\bar X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) exactly.
  \end{block}
\end{frame}
\section{Choosing one- and two-sample tests}

\begin{frame}{One sample: tests of a mean}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{One-sample \(t\)-test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:\mu\ne\mu_0\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent observations from a normal population.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle T=\frac{\bar Y-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\). Under \(H_0\), \(T\sim t_{n-1}\) exactly.
          \item \textbf{Use:} a small sample when the target is a mean.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} exact under normality; skewness and outliers matter with small \(n\).
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Large-sample \(z\)-test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:\mu\ne\mu_0\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent observations, finite variance, and sufficiently large \(n\).
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle Z_n=\frac{\bar Y-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt n}\). Under \(H_0\), \(Z_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\), or \(Z_{n}^2\xrightarrow{d}\chi_{(1)}^2\).
          \item \textbf{Use:} a large sample; raw outcomes need not be normal.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} flexible via the CLT; accuracy can suffer with severe skew or heavy tails.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why can the CLT approximation struggle?}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{block}{Berry--Esseen theorem}
    If \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\) are iid with mean \(\mu\), variance
    \(0<\sigma^2<\infty\), and \(\rho_3=\E|Y_i-\mu|^3<\infty\), then
    \[
      \sup_{x\in\mathbb R}
      \left|\Pr\!\left(\frac{\sqrt n(\bar Y-\mu)}{\sigma}\le x\right)
      -\Phi(x)\right|
      \le \frac{C\rho_3}{\sigma^3\sqrt n},
    \]
    where \(C\) is a universal constant. A large \(\rho_3/\sigma^3\)
    gives a weaker finite-sample guarantee.
  \end{block}
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Absolute moment and skewness}
        \(\displaystyle
          \gamma_1=\frac{\E[(Y-\mu)^3]}{\sigma^3},
          \qquad |\gamma_1|\leq\frac{\rho_3}{\sigma^3}.
        \)
        Thus large absolute skewness makes the Berry-Esseen bound large
        at a fixed sample size.
      \end{exampleblock}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{alertblock}{Cauchy counterexample}
        If \(Y_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Cauchy}(0,1)\), then
        CLT does not apply, because a Cauchy distribution does not have
        a finite variance.
      \end{alertblock}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ways to handle nonnormal data}
  \large
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Use a large-sample method.
    \item Use a nonparametric method.
    \item Use a bootstrap method.
    \item Transform the outcome.
    \item Use a nonnormal distribution that fits the data.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{One sample: tests of a median}
  \footnotesize
  Let \(m_0\) be the proposed population median.
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Sign test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:m=m_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:m\ne m_0\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent observations from a continuous distribution; no symmetry needed.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} count observations above \(m_0\); under \(H_0\), the count is \(\operatorname{Binomial}(n,1/2)\).
          \item \textbf{Use:} a median question with skewed or outlier-prone data.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} robust, but discards magnitude and can have low power.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Wilcoxon signed-rank test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:m=m_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:m\ne m_0\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent observations from a distribution symmetric about its median.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} rank \(\lvert Y_i-m_0\rvert\), then sum ranks with positive signs.
          \item \textbf{Use:} nonnormal but approximately symmetric data.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} uses more information than signs, and hence has higher power when the assumption is valid; symmetry is essential.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{1mm}
  %\scriptsize For exact procedures, handle observations equal to \(m_0\) and tied ranks as specified by the method.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two independent samples: small-sample mean tests}
  \footnotesize
  Both test \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=\delta_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:\mu_1-\mu_2\ne\delta_0\).
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Pooled two-sample \(t\)-test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent normal samples with equal population variances.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle T_p=\frac{\bar Y_1-\bar Y_2-\delta_0}{s_p\sqrt{1/n_1+1/n_2}}\). Under \(H_0\), \(T_p\sim t_{n_1+n_2-2}\) exactly.
          \item \textbf{Use:} two unrelated, roughly normal groups when equal variances are credible.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} efficient if variances agree; misleading standard error if they differ.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Welch two-sample \(t\)-test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent normal samples; variances may differ.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle T_W=\frac{\bar Y_1-\bar Y_2-\delta_0}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}\). Under \(H_0\), use an approximate \(t_{\widehat\nu_W}\) reference, with Welch--Satterthwaite degrees of freedom.
          \item \textbf{Use:} two unrelated, roughly normal groups with unequal or uncertain variances.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} avoids equal-variance assumption; small-sample reference is approximate.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{1mm}
  \scriptsize Here \(s_p^2=\bigl[(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2\bigr]/(n_1+n_2-2)\).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two independent samples: large means or variances}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Large-sample \(z\)-test for means}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=\delta_0\) vs.\ \(H_A:\mu_1-\mu_2\ne\delta_0\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent groups; both samples large with finite variances.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle Z=\frac{\bar Y_1-\bar Y_2-\delta_0}{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}}\). Under \(H_0\), \(Z\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\).
          \item \textbf{Use:} comparing means when normality or equal variances are doubtful.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} broad applicability; still relies on large-sample approximation.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{\(F\)-test for equality of variances}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\) vs.\ \(H_A:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2\).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent normal samples.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} \(\displaystyle F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}\). Under \(H_0\), \(F\sim F_{n_1-1,n_2-1}\) exactly.
          \item \textbf{Use:} when the variances themselves are the scientific target.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} exact under normality; extremely sensitive to nonnormality;
                                   low power for small samples; high type I error for large samples.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two independent samples: ranks or randomization}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Wilcoxon rank-sum / Mann--Whitney test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:F_1(y)=F_2(y)\); \(H_A:F_1(y + \Delta)=F_2(y)\), where \(\Delta \neq 0\) (a nonzero location shift).
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent samples; similar shapes for a pure location interpretation.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} pool and rank all outcomes; sum the ranks in one group.
          \item \textbf{Use:} a nonnormal, two-group location comparison.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} avoids normality; a difference may reflect shape or spread, not only medians.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Permutation test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} under the null, assignment changes no participant's outcome.
          \item \textbf{Assume:} treatment labels are exchangeable under \(H_0\), as justified by random assignment.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} choose a contrast, e.g.\ \(\bar Y_1-\bar Y_2\); recompute it over assignments allowed by the trial design.
          \item \textbf{Use:} a randomized two-group study, especially with small samples.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} finite-sample randomization inference; result depends on the chosen contrast.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Whole distributions and paired observations}
  \footnotesize
  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov test}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} \(H_0:F_1(y)=F_2(y)\) for every \(y\); \(H_A\): they differ somewhere.
          \item \textbf{Assume:} independent samples from continuous distributions.
          \item \textbf{Statistic:} largest vertical gap between the two empirical CDFs.
          \item \textbf{Use:} asking whether the entire distributions differ.
          \item \textbf{Tradeoff:} detects shape changes; less targeted for a specific mean or median, and ties complicate standard calibration.
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.49\textwidth}
      \begin{exampleblock}{Paired data: first take differences}
        For each pair, define \(D_i=Y_{i,\mathrm{after}}-Y_{i,\mathrm{before}}\).
        Then ask a \emph{one-sample} question about the \(D_i\):
        \begin{itemize}
          \item mean, normal differences: one-sample \(t\)-test;
          \item mean, many differences: large-sample \(z\)-test;
          \item median, asymmetric differences: sign test;
          \item median, symmetric differences: signed-rank test.
        \end{itemize}
        %For the paired \(t\)-test, \(\displaystyle T_D=\frac{\bar D-\delta_0}{s_D/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\) exactly under \(H_0\) if differences are normal.

        \textbf{Check assumptions on the differences}, not on each occasion separately.
      \end{exampleblock}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practice: which test would you use? (1)}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Twelve patients have blood pressure measured before and after a program.
      The within-patient differences are strongly right-skewed. The question is
      whether the \emph{median difference} is zero. Which test would you use?
    \vspace{5mm}
    \item Two unrelated treatment groups each contain about 15 patients.
      Outcomes look roughly normal, but one group's spread is much larger.
      The question is whether the \emph{population means} differ.
      Which test would you use?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practice: which test would you use? (2)}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \setcounter{enumi}{2}
    \item In a small randomized trial, eight participants receive each treatment.
      The outcome is highly skewed. You want to use the difference in sample means
      and the actual treatment assignment to test for an effect.
      Which test would you use?
    \vspace{5mm}
    \item Two independent groups have similar sample means, but the investigator
      suspects that their \emph{overall outcome distributions} differ in shape.
      Outcomes are continuous. Which test would you use?
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Practice answers}
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sign test on the paired differences.}
      The target is the median difference, and the differences are strongly skewed;
      the sign test does not require symmetry.
    \vspace{2mm}
    \item \textbf{Welch two-sample \(t\)-test.}
      The groups are independent and the target is a difference in means;
      Welch allows unequal variances.
    \vspace{2mm}
    \item \textbf{Permutation test using the difference in means.}
      Reassign treatment labels according to the trial's randomization scheme
      to obtain the null distribution.
    \vspace{2mm}
    \item \textbf{Two-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov test.}
      The question concerns the entire distributions, including possible
      differences in shape despite similar means.
  \end{enumerate}
